## Path of the Martial God — the whole game in one document

*Read this to understand the game. Read the other 22 documents to build it.*

---

## The pitch

A third-person martial-arts action RPG in which you are raised from the age of nine by a man who once broke sixty-three teachers to prevent a war — and succeeded — and who is trying to find out whether the restraint he never had can be taught.

**Genre:** third-person martial-arts action RPG · **Platform:** PC lead, PS5/Xbox Series · **Length:** 20–24h · **Frame rate:** 60fps, as a design constraint, not a polish goal · **Rating:** M/18

---

## 1. The one idea everything comes from

> **A martial god is someone who can end any fight and chooses how it ends.**

Mastery is not more damage. Mastery is **more terminal options**.

- A beginner can only flail until a bar empties. One available ending.
- A master standing over a broken opponent can break the arm, take the back and choke them out, dismantle their base until they cannot continue, submit them, humiliate them, kill them — or **stop one inch short and let them feel it.**

Each is a distinct mechanical act with distinct animation, cost, and consequence. The moment you choose is called **the Final Inch**: time dilates for about a second, the camera shows both faces, no prompt explains anything, and there is no undo.

**Three things fall out of this single idea:**

1. **Progression is perceptible without a menu** — you know you got better because you have choices you did not have before.
2. **Morality needs no meter** — the moral system's input is a fighting decision made at fighting speed. Character is what you do when there is no time to perform being good.
3. **The mentor relationship and the morality system are the same system** — Ruhn took you specifically to find out what you do in that moment. Every training hour is him running the experiment. The ending is the result.

**And one rule that makes it all work:** *the Stop is a skill.* A novice who tries to pull a punch fails — the technique is rough, the motion overshoots, and they connect anyway. **The ability to be merciful is mechanically gated behind competence.** Restraint is not available to the weak. That is the whole game as a single rule.

## 2. The feature test

Every proposal is checked against one question:

> **Does this widen the space of things the player can decide to do to another person?**

If it only makes the player stronger, it's a stat, and stats are the failure mode this game exists to avoid.

## 3. Combat in one page

**Three resources, three ways to win.** Styles have opinions about which you pursue, and NPCs comment on *how* you win, not just that you did.

| Resource | What it is | Losing it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Vitality** | Accumulated trauma, in six body regions. Doesn't fully restore between fights. | Unconscious. Never automatically dead. |
| **Structure** | **A base with four quadrants**, not a bar. Force degrades a direction; you can't bear weight where it's broken. | Dumped off your base. The Final Inch opens. |
| **Breath** | Spent by *inefficiency and panic*, not by dashing. Rough techniques cost far more than mastered ones. | Gassed — the most vulnerable state in the game, entirely self-inflicted. |

**Why quadrants matter:** a bar makes defence a rhythm game. Quadrants make it *spatial* — where the pressure comes from, which way you're being walked, which direction you have left. It's why footwork exists mechanically, and it's what makes the styles genuinely different, because each attacks the base a different way.

**Seven verbs:** strike, commit, guard, deflect, evade, seize, stance — plus **Focus** (breathe, recover, read, and be vulnerable). No combo strings. No move list. Nothing to memorise.

**The core innovation — the intent grammar.** The left stick supplies *intent*: **pressure, retreat, angle, close**. Each style maps every (verb × intent) pair to a different, philosophically consistent technique.

> **The inputs never change. The meaning of the inputs changes.**

*Strike + Angle* is a shoulder-first re-entry in Low River, a switch-kick in Split Reed, an arm-drag to the back in Standing Water, a pivot-throw in Glass Hour. The player learns a **language of intent**; each style is a **dialect**. That's what makes the endgame possible — fluency in intent means you can change dialect mid-sentence, and eventually stop speaking any named dialect at all.

**No HUD by default.** You read the opponent's body: injury for Vitality, posture for Structure, **breathing for Breath** (audio is the stamina bar), eyes and distance for Will.

## 4. Four styles and the Quiet

Each owns a unique position in *(resource attacked × distance)*. They form a **non-transitive web**, not a power ladder.

| Style | Attacks | Range | Signature | Philosophy |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Low River** | Structure | Mid | **The Line** — advancing straight into the centre stacks power; retreating drops it all | Take the centre and walk through the man |
| **Split Reed** | Vitality | Long | **The Measure** — acting from the exact edge of range is faster and safer | Never be where he is aiming |
| **Standing Water** | Structure | Contact | **Custody** — a grip graph where you hold a person entirely in your power | A fight is a negotiation about where a body may be |
| **Glass Hour** | Borrowed structure | Reactive | **The Borrow** — deflecting *stores* their force to spend on a throw or a break | Do nothing first. Add to what already moves |
| **The Quiet** | — | Any | **Accord** — a *correct* technique makes the next one free, from any lineage | You are no longer using an art. You are answering the question. |

**Glass Hour has an anti-mechanic built in, and it's the best thing about it:** an opponent who commits to nothing gives you nothing. Against a patient fighter, the art is nearly helpless — which is the extinction of the mountain temples, expressed as a mechanic.

**The Quiet is the martial god, mechanically.** You give up every signature mechanic. In exchange, your *Instinctive* techniques become available outside their style's grammar, selected by the situation rather than by a binding. A player who doesn't understand the fight is **worse** in the Quiet than a competent stylist. A player who does is doing something no style can do. **Skill, not stats.**

**Your identity is produced by constraint.** Your **body** — three paired sliders (Root↔Mobility, Density↔Speed, Wind↔Burst) under a hard budget — determines which styles you can push to the top. You can learn everything and master only some. Plus permanent injuries redirect you. A playthrough has a *shape*.

## 5. How you learn — the Ledger

**No XP tree.** A tree makes every technique a purchase against a currency, which destroys the thing the game wants most: that you remember where each thing you know came from.

Instead: a physical notebook — **which is also the entire menu system** — where every technique carries a **provenance line**.

| Channel | How |
| --- | --- |
| **Taught** | Someone drills you |
| **Stolen** | It was used *against you* enough times. **You learn best what beats you.** |
| **Earned** | You beat someone who used it |
| **Derived** | You defended it enough to find the counter. It's yours, and **you name it** |
| **Inherited** | From the dead, from a lineage, from a teacher who has decided to stop |

> *"Shoulder-check — the barge dock, age twelve. A rail guard whose name I never got. He did it to me six times."*

**Mastery:** Impression → Learned → Sound → Silent → Instinctive. What improves is startup, recovery, Breath cost, cancels, options — **never a damage percentage**. At *Instinctive* a technique gains its **Final Inch terminal**: mastery literally widening the number of ways you can choose to end a person.

**You cannot grind.** Advancement is weighted by threat, novelty, correctness, and pressure. Repeating one technique on one enemy type yields nothing. **Drills cap at Sound, permanently.** You must fight. And *Instinctive* must be proved once, at the decisive moment of a real fight — the Ledger records which one.

**Training is playable and every drill teaches a real mechanic.** Falling (the recovery system — the first lesson in the game is how to be defeated safely). The Base (quadrants). The Rope (distance). Hands (deflect). The Count (timing). Forms (economy of motion, graded honestly). **Restriction sparring** — *he uses only one style; you may only win by structure; you may not retreat past this line* — is cheap to author and the best teacher in the design.

**Meditation** is the fight recorder replayed with annotation: where you lost your base, where you held your breath, what you overused, and **what your opponent learned about you.** One feature serving as tutorial, analytics, adaptive-AI discovery, character moment, and dev debugger.

## 6. Opponents who learn

**The Read.** Significant opponents build a tendency table — your opening, your favoured range, your dodge bias, your favourite combo, your preferred terminal — and **buy counters** when confidence crosses a threshold.

The rules that make it fair, all enforced architecturally:
- **No input reading, ever.** Perception is latency-gated (180–380ms) and structurally cannot reach the input buffer.
- **Every adaptation is preceded by a visible tell.** You can read the read. Elite play is counter-counter play.
- **Adaptations decay.** Changing works — that's the lesson the system exists to teach.
- **Feints poison it.** A Lie feeds false tendencies. One system attacking another.
- **Never modifies stats.** Behaviour weights only.

**Rivals carry their read table across the entire campaign.** Yeo Ansa remembers how you fought at sixteen when she meets you at twenty-four. One serialised table per character; the highest value-per-byte feature in the game.

## 7. The world

**Not feudal Japan.** The **Concord**: a nine-province empire that fractured forty-one years ago, replaced not by a state but by **a railway, a telegraph line, and a set of contracts.**

With no court powerful enough to settle disputes, **the martial schools became the institutions.** Escort houses guard freight. Arbitration houses settle civil disputes by proxy duel, with a written result. Fighters are **civil infrastructure**, and a school's reputation is a commercial asset.

This answers "why do martial artists matter" with an economy rather than a vibe — and it means a martial god isn't a legend, it's a **systemic problem**, which is exactly Ruhn's argument.

Late-19th-century parity. Coal smoke, rail ballast, quilted cotton, oil lamps giving way to arc lights, river barges, telegraph offices, and mountain temples that predate all of it. Firearms exist but are expensive, licensed, and socially disastrous — a man who draws one has admitted he can't settle it himself. **Visually unoccupied. Nobody has made this martial-arts game.**

**Five factions, no good school and no evil school** — each an institution with an economic function, a defensible worldview, an internal split, and a grievance with Ruhn. The **Ninth Bell** (arbitration: violence must be a court). The **Iron Road** (escort work: a technique that doesn't work in a freight car is decoration). The **Thousand Steps** (the dying temples: the art is an end in itself). **The Cut** (the mining-town pit circuit — not evil, *desperate*, and the best raw talent in the world). **The Vessel** (the new synthesising school: right about a great deal, with no idea what it's throwing away).

## 8. Morality without a meter

**No karma bar. No virtue points. No label of any kind.** Instead, a two-layer model:

```
CONDUCT → RECORD (facts + witnesses) → RUMOUR NETWORK → STANDING (beliefs) → THE WORLD
```

> **What you did ≠ what people know.**

The Record is objective and permanent, and it logs **who saw it**. Standing is what specific factions, settlements, and people *believe*, assembled only from rumours they actually received — travelling along real channels at real speeds with real distortion. The Cut exaggerates *deliberately*, because a frightening fighter sells tickets. The registry records outcomes but never conduct. The high country hears almost nothing, so they judge on what they see.

**You can outrun your reputation, and it will arrive** — two chapters later, wearing a different shape.

**Why it can't be gamed:** it responds to *ratios and patterns*, never single acts. **Costly acts outweigh free ones by an order of magnitude.** Restraint *under provocation* is weighted ten times ordinary mercy. Farming good deeds where nobody watches does nothing. And there is **no preview and no feedback** — you find out from the world, weeks later, when a door is open or closed.

**Your use-name is the reputation readout** — diegetic, plural, and not under your control. The Cut calls you one thing, the registry writes another, your friend calls you something else. You learn what you are by hearing what people call you.

**And Ruhn is off the network.** He receives no rumours — only what he has seen and what three trusted people tell him. So a player who performs virtue publicly and cruelty privately finds the world adoring and **Ruhn unmoved**; a player quietly decent where nobody watches finds the world calls them a butcher and **Ruhn knows better.** This asymmetry is why the ending is earned rather than tallied.

## 9. Ruhn

**Age 68 at the prologue, 86 at the end. He mends nets and is paid in fish.**

He was the finest fighter of the imperial Arbitration Corps — the body that settled disputes by proxy duel so that armies would not march — undefeated for eleven years, sincerely believing his skill was public service. When the empire fell, he was ordered to break the schools that would not accept the settlement. Over twenty-six months he crippled **sixty-three fighters**, most of them teachers, most in front of their own students, in fair fights.

**And it worked. There was no war.**

There was also no one left who could teach the old arts properly, and the generation that followed learned fighting as a trade. He won the peace and destroyed the thing the peace was for.

> **Ruhn's crime is that he was right.**

He has refused every student for forty years. Then a nine-year-old loses a fight, stands over someone who is already down, has a free shot — **and pauses.**

That pause is the one thing Ruhn has never had. He crippled sixty-three people and never hesitated once, and he has spent forty years unable to decide whether that was a virtue he had or a component he was missing. So he takes a student, and what he is actually doing is running an experiment:

> **Can the brake be taught? Does restraint survive capability?**

If it can, he was a solvable problem and sixty-three people are his fault. If it can't, he was never going to be anything else — and neither is the child, and the art must end.

**No hidden evil. No betrayal. No secret crime.** Everything he did was public, ordered, reasoned, and effective. The horror is that it's all on the record and it all makes sense.

**He never asks the player for anything** — and every other teacher in the world wants money, service, deference, or a lineage. That asymmetry is the ache at the centre of the game.

## 10. The ending

By the last chapter you're strong enough that the world organises around you. Schools want your endorsement. A rival uses your reputation to justify what they do to strangers. **You have become the strongest argument in the room** — and whatever your philosophy is, other people now enforce it on strangers with their fists.

Ruhn recognises it exactly, because it happened to him, at the same age, for the same reasons.

> **"A person who cannot be answered cannot be corrected. It does not matter what you believe. It matters that there is no longer anything in the world that can tell you no. I was not wrong about the war. I was never wrong. That is what I am telling you."**

He has come to **end the lineage.**

**The penultimate boss is Kem** — your friend from the vertical slice, who had no philosophy of his own and therefore absorbed yours. He has forty students now, in a mining town where everyone loves him, learning your habits and your terminals at third hand from a man who could never explain why. His build is derived from your own telemetry. The fight is not against evil; it's against consequence. *Look what you made* — then *and here's why that's dangerous.*

**The final fight removes every crutch.** He doesn't commit, so Glass Hour has nothing to borrow. He doesn't chase, so Split Reed can't make distance. He can't be moved, so Standing Water gets nothing. He invented Low River. And his Read has been building a model of you **since you were nine, in fiction and in the save file.** Three phases: your first language used against you by its author; fragments of Glass Hour learned *from the people he broke*; and then he drops his stance into the Quiet — and you recognise the posture of a man mending nets eighteen years ago.

**Five endings, none selected from a wheel.** The terminal you take is checked *against your record* — a player whose history contradicts their final gesture doesn't get the ending they reached for, because Ruhn reads it as a performance and he isn't wrong.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| **The Answer** | The Stop, backed by a record of costly restraint. He gets the result he couldn't obtain in forty years — which means he *could* have been different, and sixty-three people are his fault. The last shot is his face working that out. |
| **The Inheritance** | You kill or break him. **Not the bad ending** — framed as: he was right. |
| **The Refusal** | Win by exhaustion and decline every terminal. He doesn't accept it. You don't need him to. |
| **The Lineage** | You lose, having never reached for a terminal you had. He wins, and finds too late that the thing he was looking for was there. |
| **Stillwater** | The Stop, plus students of your own, plus Kem surviving. The art continues, changed, in hands that were given reasons instead of habits. The last scene is the *Hands* drill, in the yard, with someone else's child. |

**And the gate that makes it honest:** the two best endings require the Stop, and **the Stop is gated behind mastery.** A player who can't pull a strike can't choose mercy at the last instant. They will connect.

## 11. Look and sound

**Grounded stylisation** — realistic anatomy (it's a game about bodies), stylised matte surfacing. Hand-tinted early photography meeting ink wash, dragged through coal smoke.

**Red is reserved.** Blood and lacquer are the only saturated reds in most scenes, so violence is chromatically loud even when quiet, and a split lip reads across a wide shot.

**Your silhouette is your portrait** — it changes with your build, so a rooted grappler and a mobile kicker are different people from across a courtyard. **Your coat is a map of your life**: worn for the whole game, torn, patched, and repaired in the local style of wherever it was repaired. Zero mechanical benefit, enormous identity value.

**The Ledger is the UI.** Hand-ruled paper and ink; a technique at Impression is a scrawl with question marks, at Instinctive a clean single line. **You read your own mastery in your own handwriting.** Two typefaces, no sans-serif anywhere, no glowing rectangles.

**Impact is weight, not sparks** — frame-holds, micro-shake, cloth ripple, dust. **No particle systems on strikes at all.** The only time-dilation in the game is the Final Inch, so it always means something.

**Animation thesis: the protagonist gets better by *losing* motion.** Every core technique is authored three times — **Rough** (telegraph, over-rotation, recovery stagger), **Sound**, **Silent** (compact; lands and resets). The frame data follows the animation *honestly* — Silent is faster because the motion is genuinely shorter. And five **additive layers** (breathing, fatigue, injury, structure, will) drive off the resource values directly, which is how the body replaces the HUD.

**Audio: breathing is the interface.** No stamina bar means breath carries it — yours mixed forward, theirs by distance. **Master-tier fighters are quiet** — less cloth, less foot noise, shorter breath. The player becomes audibly quieter across eighteen years and **nobody ever mentions it.** Then they fight the quietest man in the game.

**No music in ordinary fights.** Ruhn's theme is one bowed string played by an old player; **the player's theme is the same phrase played by a younger one**, and it cleans up across the campaign in exact parallel with the animation tiers. In the final fight both play, the same phrase at different tempos. Then one stops.

## 12. What we build first

**A 45–55 minute vertical slice in Mudgate**, with both moral branches fully authored.

You carry water past an arbitration boat where two adults settle a property dispute by proxy duel while a clerk writes it down — so before you throw a punch, you learn that **violence here is paperwork.** Three older kids take your buckets and **you lose**, and the combat tutorial is a losing fight. One of them goes down, and you have a free shot, and the game gives you the **first Final Inch with no explanation of what it means.** Ruhn is watching from the levee. He offers exactly one thing: *"I will teach you to fall."*

Then fourteen minutes in the yard — three drills, three times, seasons changing, ages 9 to 12, **with your animation promoting Rough → Sound in front of you.** Then the village, where the kid from the first fight has a limp, or doesn't, and his mother looks at you and nobody says anything. You steal a Split Reed stance by watching an escort drill on a boat deck. You win your first real fight and steal a shoulder-check from the man who kept hitting you with it. You meet **Kem**, who is better than you and delighted about it.

And then Ruhn puts one hand behind his back and only defends, and **the win condition is not damage — you must land one thing he does not expect.**

You land it. He stops. Long silence. And he says the thing that reframes the whole game, which is not pleasure and not anger: he has just received the first result of an experiment you don't know you're in, it isn't what he expected, and now he has a decision to make about a twelve-year-old.

**Title card.**

## 13. Build it in Unreal, but not the Unreal way

**Unreal 5.x** — for Motion Matching, IK Rig/Retargeter (essential for one skeleton across four ages), the two-character animation tooling, and the console path. We spend Unity's better iteration speed to buy down animation risk, which is the project's top risk.

**But do not use GAS for combat.** Fighting is a deterministic, frame-authoritative system; a generic ability framework fights you on every tuning pass. Build a bespoke C++ combat core (`FormMachine`) at a fixed 60Hz, with **all frame windows in plain-text data files in the repository, never on animation notifies.**

That last decision pays three times: designers tune feel without touching animation, every change is diffable and reviewable, and the deterministic core gives us **replay for free** — which *ships as the in-game Meditation feature* and doubles as the regression-test harness and the AI debugger.

**Build order:** combat machine → quadrants and reactions → animation pipeline → tier system on three techniques → additive layers, **then turn the HUD off and check the game still works.** Custody comes last, because it's the biggest risk and must block nothing.

## 14. Timeline, and the decision

**~33 weeks to an externally-tested vertical slice** (~10 months with honest contingency), with a 4–6 person team and heavy AI assistance on code, tools, tests, data, and previz — and **a hard red line: combat animation, principal characters, and principal VO are never AI-final**, enforced by a CI check on an asset provenance manifest, because intentions don't survive deadlines and build failures do.

**The production decision rests on two questions:**

1. **Does the player immediately ask what happens next at Beat 11?** (≥70%)
2. **Can a tester identify the later of two technique clips, unprompted, without being told what to look for?** (≥80%)

If those miss, the right answer is to fix the slice or stop. **Content volume does not solve a feel problem.**

---

## 15. The three risks that actually kill this

**Animation scope**, **combat feel**, and **team scope** — and they compound: a feel problem prompts more animation, which strains the team, which slows feel iteration.

The roadmap attacks all three in the first ten weeks: art-free combat prototype first, retarget pipeline proven in week four, and a hard clip budget (~880 for V1) reported automatically in CI with a pre-agreed cut list that executes without debate.
