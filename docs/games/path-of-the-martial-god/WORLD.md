# WORLD.md
## The Concord — setting, geography, and martial culture

---

## 1. The setting decision

**Rejected: feudal Japan.** It is the genre default, it is occupied by *Ghost of Tsushima* and *Sekiro* at a level of production quality we cannot match, and it imports a ready-made honour system that would do our moral thinking for us. If the world already tells the player what honour means, `MORALITY_AND_REPUTATION.md` has nothing to do.

**Rejected: contemporary setting.** Guns collapse the premise. A world with firearms has to explain, constantly, why anyone fights with their hands — and every explanation is a contrivance (tournaments, gangland honour codes, sport). *Sifu* solves it by being a compressed revenge night; we need eighteen years.

**Rejected: timeless mythic wuxia.** Flying, glowing, qi blasts. A world with supernatural power makes "what does it mean to become a martial god" a question about magic rather than about people.

**Chosen: a fictional, East-Asian-inspired continent at the moment industry arrives and the state collapses.**

> **The Concord.** Forty-one years ago the Concordance — a nine-province empire — fractured. What replaced it was not a new state but a **railway, a telegraph line, and a set of contracts.** Cargo moves, disputes arise, and there is no court with the power to settle them. So the martial schools became the institutions: escort houses guard freight, arbitration houses settle civil disputes by proxy duel, and the schools that train those fighters hold genuine political and economic power.

**Why this is the strongest choice:**

1. **It answers "why do martial artists matter" with an economy rather than a vibe.** Fighters are civil infrastructure. A school's reputation is a commercial asset. A martial god is not a legend — a martial god is a *systemic problem*, which is precisely Ruhn's argument.
2. **The technology level is perfect.** Steam and rail connect the world — which is what makes **rumour** a designed system rather than a hand-wave (§7). Firearms exist but are expensive, unreliable, licensed, and socially disastrous to carry; a man who draws one has admitted he cannot settle it himself, and the world despises him for it. That is a *cultural* reason rather than a contrived one.
3. **It is inherently about change**, which is the game's subject. The old arts are dying because the new economy has no use for fifteen-year disciplines. Ruhn broke the teachers; industry buried them. Both happened, and the second one would have happened anyway. That ambiguity is the whole theme.
4. **Visually it is unoccupied.** Coal smoke, wet rail ballast, quilted cotton, oil lamps giving way to arc lights, river barges, telegraph offices, and mountain temples that predate all of it. Nobody has made this martial-arts game.

**Era name in-fiction:** the Concord (people named the ruin after the thing it used to be, which is a joke everybody has stopped finding funny). Approximate technology parity: late nineteenth century.

## 2. Naming conventions

Names are **invented**, not borrowed from any real culture. The system, documented here so all future writing stays consistent:

- **People:** family name (one syllable) then given name (two syllables, hyphenated in formal address, run together casually). *Ruhn Ijae-so. Yeo An-sa. Kem Du-ro. Ise Vau-ran.*
- **Use-names:** any fighter of note earns a public fighting name from their first significant victory *or defeat*. The use-name is how the world tracks reputation — it is literally the key in the rumour database. *The Tally. Bright Kem. The Ninebell Butcher.*
- **Places:** predominantly **translated and functional** — Ironmouth, the Long Water, the Thousand Steps, the Cut, Ashgate, Mudgate, the Gate of Nine Bells. This reads beautifully, avoids invented-syllable soup, and tells you what a place *does*.
- **Martial arts:** two names each — a **registry name** from the Concordance's licensing catalogue, and a **common name**. The gap between them is a theme (`MARTIAL_STYLES.md` §2).

## 3. Geography

The Concord is one temperate continent, running from a cold interior plateau down a single great river system to a working coast. Six regions matter — five of them places, and one of them a system.

### Mudgate — the Long Water, lower reaches
A river village of about four hundred people: levees, fish traps, a barge dock, a rope bridge, a shrine nobody maintains, and a mooring where the Ninth Bell's arbitration boat ties up twice a year. Brown water, green levees, grey sky. This is home, and the player will return to it three times across the game and find it different each time.

### Ironmouth — the river mouth, the rail terminus, the coast
The largest city in the Concord and the one that actually runs it. Where the Long Water meets the sea and the rail meets the water. Coal, steel, freight yards, warehouses, company offices, and the headquarters of the Iron Road. Rust and steam-white. It never stops moving and it does not care who you are unless you are on a manifest.

### The Gate of Nine Bells ("Ninebell") — the old imperial capital
A museum city. Enormous ceremonial architecture built for an empire that no longer exists, now occupied by arbitration houses, registrars, clerks, licensed duelling grounds, and the descendants of people who used to matter. Bone, brass, deep green. Beautiful, slow, and quietly vicious. Everything here is procedure.

### The Cut — the mining country, interior
A gash of company towns along a coal seam: Ashgate, Low Hollow, Pitcher. Coal-black and sodium-orange. Company scrip, company housing, bad air, and the **pit circuit** — the underground fight economy where the best raw talent in the world is, because it is the only place a poor fighter can eat. The Cut is not evil. The Cut is *desperate*, and it is the most alive place in the game.

### The Thousand Steps — the high country
Above the treeline. Monastic lineages that predate the Concordance by centuries, reached by a stair cut into the mountain. Fog-white, slate, and a single vermilion. Perhaps two hundred people still live up there, most of them old. They have the best art in the world and nobody to give it to.

### The Iron Road — the connective tissue
Not a place but a **system**: the rail line, the river route, the stations, the freight cars, the water stops, the telegraph poles. The player spends real time on it, and it functions as the game's corridor between regions. Fights on moving trains and barges are a distinct encounter category with their own footing rules.

## 4. Travel

Travel is **routed, paid, and temporal**. You take a train, a barge, or you walk. Each takes in-game time, costs money or an obligation, and *is a place* — carriages, decks, and waystations are playable spaces with encounters, conversations, and the occasional fight in a moving box.

**No fast travel in V1 beyond re-riding a route you have already opened**, and even that costs days. Time passing is a mechanic (`PROGRESSION.md` §8, `MORALITY_AND_REPUTATION.md` §3 — rumours travel at the speed of the rail, so *distance from your own reputation is real*).

## 5. Social hierarchy

Five strata, and martial skill cuts across all of them, which is why it matters:

1. **Company and contract holders** — the people who own the rail, the mines, and the freight. New money, no lineage, immense power.
2. **The registered** — licensed fighters, arbiters, escort houses, school heads. The professional martial class. Respectable, credentialed, and increasingly for sale.
3. **Working people** — bargemen, miners, clerks, farmers, rail crews. The overwhelming majority, and the people the player's conduct actually affects.
4. **The lineages** — old families and old temples with prestige and no money. Ise Vauran's people. Declining with enormous dignity.
5. **The unregistered** — pit fighters, drifters, the Cut's population, anyone without papers. The best fighters in the world are disproportionately here.

**Martial skill is the only reliable ladder between strata**, which is why everyone in this world has an opinion about fighters and why a martial god is a genuinely destabilising event.

## 6. Martial culture

- **Fighting is a trade, and it is licensed.** The Concordance's registry survived the Concordance. To fight for money legally you need papers. Most fighters do not have them.
- **Proxy duelling is a legal instrument.** Two parties in a civil dispute may submit to arbitration by combat, overseen by a Ninth Bell arbiter, with a written result. It is real law. It is also increasingly purchasable, which is the Ninth Bell's internal crisis.
- **Use-names are public record.** The registry maintains them. So does gossip. So does the pit circuit's chalkboard.
- **Killing is a crime**, prosecutable in the larger cities and unenforceable everywhere else. The real penalty for killing is never legal; it is that everyone finds out.
- **Schools are businesses.** A school's reputation determines its student intake, its escort contracts, and its arbitration fees. Beating a school's champion in public is a *financial attack*, and the game treats it as one.
- **Teaching is a status.** To have students is to have a lineage, which is the only form of immortality this world offers. That is why the last act of the game is about who, if anyone, the player teaches.

## 7. Information travels — the rumour infrastructure

This is a **system**, not flavour, and it is the backbone of `MORALITY_AND_REPUTATION.md`.

| Channel | Speed | Distortion | Reach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Telegraph** | Same day | Low (short, factual, expensive per word) | Station to station only — Ironmouth, Ninebell, the big Cut towns |
| **Rail and river crews** | Days | Medium — improves with telling | Everywhere the line goes |
| **The pit circuit** | Days to weeks | **High** — the Cut exaggerates deliberately, because a frightening fighter sells tickets | The Cut, then outward |
| **The registry** | Weeks | None, but only records *outcomes*, never conduct | The registered classes |
| **Letters** | Weeks | Low, but personal and selective | Individuals |
| **The high country** | Months, or never | — | The Thousand Steps hears almost nothing, which is why they will judge you on what they see |

**The design consequence:** the player can outrun their reputation by moving, and it will catch up. A thing done in the Cut arrives in Ninebell two chapters later, wearing a different shape. A thing done in an empty room on the Long Water may never arrive anywhere at all.

## 8. Population persistence

**The world has a finite, persistent population of named and semi-named fighters,** and this is a hard architectural requirement (`TECHNICAL_ARCHITECTURE.md` §8), not a nice-to-have.

- Every opponent the player fights above the "crowd" tier is a **persistent entity** with a record: who they are, what happened, what you did to them, whether they healed.
- They re-appear. A man whose knee you destroyed in the Cut in Chapter 2 is working a freight gate at Ironmouth in Chapter 5, and he remembers, and he has told people.
- Rivals carry their **Read table** across the whole campaign (`COMBAT_SYSTEM.md` §11).
- Budget: approximately **90 persistent fighters** in V1, of whom ~22 are named characters with dialogue and ~9 are bosses. The rest are recurring faces with a name, a record, and two or three lines.

**Why it is worth the cost:** it is the entire proof of Pillar 3 and Pillar 4. Without it, brutality has no consequences and mercy has no witnesses, and every fight is against a spawn.

## 9. Places the player will fight

Authored fight spaces, each with distinct footing, architecture, and witnesses:

| Space | Footing | Architecture | Witnesses |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Ruhn's yard, Mudgate** | Packed earth, seasonal mud | Open, one post, a wall | One |
| **The barge dock** | Wet planks, moving deck | Edges, water, cargo | Working crews |
| **The rope bridge** | Unstable, continuous rear-quadrant degradation | Nothing to brace on | None |
| **A pit at Ashgate** | Dirt, blood, sawdust | Circular, walled, no exit | A paying crowd that knows your use-name |
| **A freight car, moving** | Lurching, narrow | Crates, a door, a coupling | None, and that matters |
| **Ninebell duelling ground** | Perfect, swept stone | Formal, marked, symmetrical | Arbiters, clerks, and a written record |
| **The Thousand Steps stair** | Wet stone, vertical | Fall hazard on one side | Monks who will not intervene |
| **An Ironmouth warehouse** | Concrete and coal dust | Pillars, stacks, poor light | Whoever is on shift |
| **Stillwater** | (final) — see `STORY.md` §8 | | |

Witness composition is a mechanical property of the space (`MORALITY_AND_REPUTATION.md` §2). **Where you fight determines what the world learns.**

## 10. What the world does NOT have

- No magic, no qi projection, no flight, no supernatural anything.
- No monsters. Every opponent is a person. (Two dogs. They are extremely dangerous and cannot be lied to.)
- No open world. Hand-built regions — five in V1, plus the Iron Road as connective tissue — connected by routes, entered and left deliberately (`VERTICAL_SLICE.md` §1).
- No prophecy, no bloodline, no chosen one.
- No empire to restore. The Concordance is not coming back and nobody in the game is trying to bring it back. The world has moved on; the question is what the arts become now.
