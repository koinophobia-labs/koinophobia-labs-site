# STORY.md
## Dramatic architecture

**This document locks structure, not script.** Scene text, dialogue, and prose are written at story-lock, after design-lock. What is fixed here is the sequence of dramatic events, what each one is *for*, and what the player's conduct is allowed to change.

---

## 1. The spine in one paragraph

A man who once broke sixty-three teachers to prevent a war — and succeeded, and thereby destroyed the thing he was preserving — spends forty years refusing students, then takes a nine-year-old because he sees them hesitate over someone who was already down. He raises them to be dangerous in order to find out whether the restraint he himself never had can be taught. It can, or it cannot. The player answers that question with eighteen years of conduct, and then answers it a second time with one input, with him finished at their feet, at the exact moment when there is nothing left in the world that can tell them no.

## 2. The theme, stated as a question the game refuses to answer

> **Should a person who cannot be answered be allowed to exist?**

Ruhn says no, and has proof. The player is the counter-argument, and the only place they can make it is in the one language both of them speak.

The game takes no position. It records one.

---

## 3. Act structure

Seven chapters plus a prologue, in three movements.

| | Chapters | Age | Movement |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **I — The Yard** | Prologue, 1, 2 | 9–16 | Being made |
| **II — The World** | 3, 4, 5 | 19–24 | Being used, and finding out what made you |
| **III — The Answer** | 6, 7 | 26–27 | Becoming the problem, and being answered |

---

## 4. Movement I — The Yard

### Prologue — Mudgate (age 9) · *the inciting incident*
*This is the vertical slice (`VERTICAL_SLICE.md`).*

A river village. A Ninth Bell arbitration boat is moored, and the player watches two adults settle a property dispute by proxy duel while clerks write it down. **Violence is paperwork here, and the child sees that before they ever throw a punch.**

The player loses a fight to three older children. One goes down. The player has a free shot.

**The pause.** The first Final Inch in the game, offered with no explanation of what it means.

Ruhn, mending nets, sees it. He offers one thing: *"I will teach you to fall."*

### Chapter 1 — The Yard (ages 12–15) · *the childhood arc*
Three years compressed into roughly two and a half hours of playable training, structured as seasons. Drills, sparring, forms, the Ledger, the first stolen technique, the first time the player beats someone who is not a child.

**The load-bearing scene:** a **first serious victory** that the player does not enjoy. They win a village dispute by proxy — legitimately, for a neighbour, under the rules — and put a grown man on the ground in front of his family. It is legal, it is correct, it is what they were trained for, and it feels awful. Ruhn says nothing about it and sets a new drill the next morning. The drill is *Hands*, the gentlest one.

**Ends with:** Ruhn sending the player away. Not as a graduation — as an interruption. He says they are not ready and that this is why they must go. The player is fifteen and furious.

### Chapter 2 — Down the Water (age 16) · *the first serious defeat*
The Long Water to the Cut. Ashgate, the pits, company scrip, bad air, the best fighters the player has ever seen, and **Kem Du-ro**, who is better than them and delighted about it.

The player fights for money for the first time. They earn a **use-name**, chosen by the crowd, and they do not get a say in it.

**The first serious defeat:** an authored loss against a pit fighter whose whole method is *distance* — a Split Reed specialist the player literally cannot reach. The player loses badly and publicly, and the loss opens the Rope drill, grants Impressions, and is written into the Ledger by principle (*"Beaten by distance"*). **The game's answer to failure is a curriculum.**

**Ends with:** Yeo Ansa, who watched the fight, telling the player what they did wrong with complete accuracy and no kindness whatsoever.

## 5. Movement II — The World

### Chapter 3 — The Iron Road (age 19) · *compromise, and the betrayal*
Escort work. Honest wages, freight cars, wet rail ballast, and a slow sequence of small moral compromises that each individually make sense.

Second style opens properly. The player is now *good*, and discovers that being good at violence makes you **useful to people whose goals are not yours** — the phase's entire thesis, delivered through work rather than speeches.

**The betrayal:** **Nas O-rin** sells the player's route. People die. He is caught, delivered to the player unarmed and crying, and nobody else will decide what happens to him (`RIVALS.md` §4).

**This is the highest-weighted Record event in the game.** It propagates for the rest of the campaign, it reaches Ruhn through Yeo Ansa, and Kem will cite it — sincerely, as an example of what kind of person the player is — in Chapter 6.

**Also here:** the first **permanent injury**, incurred in the ambush. It is designed as character (`COMBAT_SYSTEM.md` §12) and it redirects the player's fighting identity for the rest of their life.

### Chapter 4 — The Gate of Nine Bells (age 22) · *institutions, and the file*
Ninebell. Bone, brass, deep green, and procedure. Licensed duelling, arbitration as a legal instrument, **Ise Vau-ran**, and the discovery that the Ninth Bell's rules are being purchased.

The player fights inside rules for the first time — scored, witnessed, documented — which is a genuinely different combat experience and which produces the game's cleanest examination of what "fair" means.

**The discovery:** in the registry, a corrected entry. **Han Su-ye** decides how much to show them. The player learns their teacher has a file.

**The confrontation:** the player goes back to Mudgate and asks. **Ruhn does not deny anything and does not explain.** He answers three factual questions truthfully and refuses the fourth, and then asks whether the player would like to train. The refusal is worse than a lie.

### Chapter 5 — The Thousand Steps (age 24) · *the truth, and the major loss*
The high country. Fog, slate, vermilion, and two hundred mostly-old people holding the best art in the world with nobody to give it to.

**Nas Il-ke tells the player everything**, over tea, without malice, as though it were common knowledge: the sixty-three, the orders, the war that did not happen, and the seven Glass Hour teachers he took from her generation. And then the worst fact in the game: **Ruhn learned Glass Hour afterward, from the people he broke.** He is the last living carrier of parts of an art he personally destroyed.

Glass Hour becomes available here — **if the player's Standing has not already arrived ahead of them** (`MORALITY_AND_REPUTATION.md`). The most valuable door in the game is one the player may have closed two chapters ago without knowing.

**The major loss:** authored, and it is not Ruhn. Depending on trajectory it is Kem's innocence, Yeo Ansa's judgement, Nas Orin's outcome catching up, or — in the most common configuration — **the player's own reputation arriving somewhere it cannot be corrected**, and a person they cared about believing it.

**Ends with:** the player understanding their entire childhood differently, and understanding that they were an experiment.

## 6. Movement III — The Answer

### Chapter 6 — What You Made (age 26) · *consequence*
The player returns to the Cut and finds that **their doctrine has students.**

**Kem Du-ro** has a yard full of them in Ashgate, where everyone loves him. They are learning the player's habits, the player's entries, and above all **the player's terminals**, at third hand, from a man who has never been able to explain why.

Whatever the player has spent eighteen years doing to defeated people, there are now forty young fighters in a mining town doing it, cheerfully, to strangers.

The chapter is a slow accumulation of **being recognised** — schools wanting endorsement, escort houses wanting the name, the Cut's chalkboard, a rival invoking the player's example to justify what they do, and civilians crossing the street. The world is organising around the player, and the game lets them feel it for three hours before anyone names it.

**The penultimate boss: Kem.** The fight is not against evil. It is against consequence — the player's own argument, worn by someone who did not choose it. His build is derived from the player's own telemetry (`RIVALS.md` §2).

At the Final Inch against Kem, whatever the player chooses, **someone is watching**, and it is the largest crowd in the game.

### Chapter 7 — Stillwater (age 27) · *the final sequence*
Ruhn comes to the player. Not a summons, not an ambush, not a betrayal. He walks into wherever they are, in his mending coat, and he is 86 and unhurried and everyone in the room can feel it.

He makes his argument, and it is not *"you have become evil."* It is:

> **"A person who cannot be answered cannot be corrected. It does not matter what you believe. It matters that there is no longer anything in the world that can tell you no. I was not wrong about the war. I was never wrong. That is what I am telling you."**

He has come to **end the lineage** — to break the player as he broke sixty-three others, and then to finish the job on himself.

**The location is his yard at Mudgate**, in the rain, on packed earth the player learned to fall on eighteen years ago, and the game has been showing them that yard in every season since they were nine.

## 7. What the player's conduct changes

**Locked (never changes):** Ruhn takes the player. The player is sent away at fifteen. Nas Orin betrays them. The file exists. Nas Il-ke tells the truth. The player's doctrine acquires students. Ruhn comes.

**Determined by conduct:**
- Which doors are open in Chapter 5 (Glass Hour, the Thousand Steps, Ise Vauran's protection).
- What Kem has become, and what the Chapter 6 fight is about.
- Yeo Ansa's trajectory, and therefore what Ruhn has been hearing for three years.
- Whether the Ninth Bell protects or prosecutes.
- The composition of the final scene's witnesses.
- **The ending.**

**Never determined by a dialogue choice.** There is no conversation in this game that selects an outcome. Conduct selects outcomes; conversation reports them.

---

## 8. The final sequence — structure

Three phases, and they are not difficulty tiers (`BOSSES.md` §12):

1. **Low River.** Your first language, used against you by the man who taught it to you. He takes the centre and walks you backward across your own childhood.
2. **What he learned.** Fragments of Glass Hour — borrowed from the dead, used by their killer. The player, if they have the art, is fighting *their own teacher's teachers*, secondhand.
3. **The Quiet.** He drops his stance. And the player realises he has been standing like that since the first hour of the game, and that the posture they finally achieved at twenty-seven is the posture an old man had while mending nets.

Then he is finished, and the game gives the player the longest Final Inch window it has, and no prompt.

## 9. Defeat as story

Losing is a branch, not a reload (`TRAINING_AND_MASTERY.md` §8, `DEATH AND FAILURE` in `OPEN_DECISIONS.md` OD-06).

- **Authored losses** exist in Ch.2 and Ch.5 and are not failures — the story continues, with an injury and a Ledger entry.
- **Some fights continue after defeat** in a worse position: you wake up somewhere, the fight resumes, a friend intervenes and now you owe them.
- **The player can lose to Kem** and the story continues, changed and worse.
- **The player can lose to Ruhn**, and that is not a game over. It is an ending (§10, *the Lineage*).

The game never signals in advance which fights are survivable losses, and it never punishes a player for winning one that was meant to be lost.

## 10. Endings

**There is no final dialogue wheel.** By the last scene the game already knows who the player became. Endings are selected by (a) the **terminal** the player takes against Ruhn and (b) the **Standing and conduct record** behind it — and crucially, **(a) is checked against (b)**. A player whose record contradicts their final gesture does not get the ending they were reaching for, because Ruhn reads it as a performance, and he is not wrong.

| Ending | Selected by | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **The Answer** | The **Stop** against Ruhn, backed by a consistent record of costly restraint | The counter-argument lands. You are unanswerable and you answered to something anyway. He gets the result he spent forty years unable to obtain, and it is not a happy ending — it means he *could* have been different, and sixty-three people are his fault. The last shot is his face working that out. |
| **The Inheritance** | **Kill** or **Break** | You become what he feared, and the world reacts exactly as he predicted. **Not framed as the bad ending.** Framed as: he was right. The most thematically complete ending and the hardest to watch. |
| **The Refusal** | Win by **Breath** (exhaustion) and decline every terminal, letting the window lapse to the end | Mechanically demanding — you must out-last an 86-year-old master without finishing him. The argument proved by not needing to make it. He does not accept it, which is the point; you do not need him to. |
| **The Lineage** | **Lose** to Ruhn in a specific way — fighting to the end without ever reaching for a terminal you had available | He wins, and finds, at the last moment, that the thing he was looking for was there the whole time and he has just destroyed it. The tragic ending. Rare, and reachable only by players who genuinely fought well and genuinely never reached. |
| **Stillwater** | The **Stop**, plus a record that includes having taken students, plus Kem surviving as *Student* or *Apart*, plus Yeo Ansa at **Partner** | The true-completion ending. The art continues, changed, in hands that were given reasons rather than habits. Ruhn's experiment concludes with an answer he can accept, and the last scene is the **Hands** drill, in the yard, with someone else's child. |

**The gate that makes this honest:** *The Answer* and *Stillwater* both require the Stop, and **the Stop is mechanically gated behind mastery** (`COMBAT_SYSTEM.md` §10). A player who cannot pull a strike cannot choose mercy at the last instant — they will connect. **The ability to be merciful is earned, not selected**, and that is the entire game stated as a rule.

---

## 11. Epilogue structure

Short, specific, and assembled from the Record rather than narrated in general terms. Not "the land knew peace." Instead: **six to ten authored cards**, selected by what actually happened, each about one person.

The man whose knee you destroyed at Ashgate. Nas Orin's sister. Kem's students, and what they teach. Whether the Thousand Steps had anyone to give it to. What the registry finally wrote under your use-name. What your brother transmitted, the last time someone asked him about you.

**The epilogue is the rumour network's final report**, and it is the only time the game ever shows the player what the world actually believed.
